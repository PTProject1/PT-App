How a muscle gets bigger

Training barely changes how many muscle fibres you have. Growth comes from making each one thicker.

A muscle is bundles inside bundles. Fascicles hold fibres, and each fibre is one long cell packed with myofibrils, the protein strands that do the contracting. Keep scrolling to zoom into one.

Scroll ↓
Signal

Tension is the trigger

When a fibre pulls hard against a load, sensors in and around its contractile strands register the mechanical tension.

That switches on mTORC1, the cell's main growth pathway, which tells ribosomes to build more contractile protein.

The burn, the pump and next-day soreness come along for the ride. Tension is the trigger with the strongest evidence behind it.

Recruitment

Only working fibres grow

Your nervous system recruits fibres in order of size, smallest first. The biggest ones, which also have the most room to grow, join only when effort is high.

That happens with a heavy load from the first rep, or in the last hard reps of a lighter set as the early fibres tire. It is why sets of roughly 5 to 30 reps build similar muscle, as long as they end close to failure.

A fibre that is never recruited never gets the signal.

Synthesis

The building window

Muscle protein is always being broken down and rebuilt. After a hard session, building speeds up. In one classic study it was up 50% at 4 hours, 109% at 24 hours, and almost back to resting level by 36.

Growth is the small surplus left when building beats breakdown. Dietary protein supplies the raw material, and the benefit levels off at around 1.6 g per kg of body weight a day.

Growth

The fibre thickens

The surplus becomes new myofibrils laid down beside the old ones, so the fibre's cross-section grows.

As it grows, satellite cells resting on the fibre's surface fuse into it and donate extra nuclei to help run the bigger cell.

Each session adds very little. Measurable size takes weeks. Early strength jumps are mostly your nervous system getting better at the lift.

Dose

More hard sets, more growth

Weekly volume matters. A pooled analysis of training studies found that muscles given 10 or more sets a week grew nearly twice as much as those given fewer than 5.

The returns diminish as volume climbs, and sets only count if they are hard enough to recruit the big fibres.

Repeat

Then do it again, slightly harder

A fibre adapts to the tension it meets. Once a load stops being hard, it stops being a strong signal.

So add a rep or a little weight when you can, sleep enough for the building to happen, and eat enough to pay for it. The loop is simple. The size comes from running it for months.
