Progress or plateau

Your muscles adapt to the work you give them. Then that work stops being enough.

A weight that took you to failure in week one leaves reps to spare by week five. An easy set sends a weak growth signal, so to keep growing the work has to keep getting a little harder.

Scroll ↓
Effort

Count the reps you had left

Reps in reserve (RIR) is how many more reps you could have done. A pooled analysis of 55 studies found that muscle growth rises as sets end closer to failure. Strength gains depended on it much less.

The app aims for 1–2 RIR on big compound lifts, where form breaks down near failure, and 0–1 on isolation exercises.

Method

Reps first, then weight

Each exercise in the app has a rep range, like 8–12. Keep the weight and add a rep where you can each session.

When every set reaches the top of the range, add the smallest weight step and start again at the bottom. This is called double progression, and it is what the app's Next target follows.

Evidence

Both kinds of progress count

In an 8-week study of 43 trained adults, one group added weight while staying at 8–12 reps, and the other added reps at the same weight.

Muscle growth was similar, and squat strength rose by about 20 kg in both. A rep gained is real progress, especially on exercises with big weight jumps.

Patience

Progress slows, and that is normal

Beginners add weight almost every session. After a few years, a couple of reps a month is good progress.

If a lift has not moved for 3–4 weeks, the app flags it. Check sleep and calories first, then consider a deload or swapping the exercise.

Tracking

Write every set down

You cannot beat last time if you do not know what you did. The app shows last session's sets on every exercise, with a Next target.

Fill in kg, reps and RIR, and the Progress screen turns them into your estimated 1RM trend for each lift.
